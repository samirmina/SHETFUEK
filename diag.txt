Most modern LLMs rely on the transformer architecture, which was introduced in the paper “Attention Is All You Need” (https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762).
The original transformer is a deep neural network designed for machine translation tasks.
Transformers consist of two submodules: an encoder and a decoder, both connected by self-attention mechanisms.
The encoder processes input text to produce numerical representations capturing contextual information. The decoder then generates output text using these vectors.

A simplified version of the transformer architecture is depicted in figure 1.4.
Transformers are used for tasks like language translation, where an encoded representation from the encoder assists the decoder to generate translated texts one word at a time.
Figure 1.4 illustrates this process with input text "This is an example" and partially translated sentence "Das ist ein."
The complete output of BERT's masked word prediction task involves generating final words given partial inputs, using the encoder submodule for representations.

Later variants like GPT and its versions build upon this architecture to adapt it for different tasks such as generative text completion or translation.

Large language models (LLMs) have transformed the field of natural language processing, which previously relied mainly on explicit rule-based systems and simpler statistical methods.
The advent of LLMs introduced new deep learning-driven approaches that led to advancements in understanding, generating, and translating human language.
Modern LLMs are trained using two main steps: pretraining on a large corpus of unlabeled text by predicting the next word in a sentence as a label, followed by fine-tuning on smaller labeled target datasets to follow instructions or perform classification tasks.
The original transformer architecture consists of an encoder for parsing text and a decoder for generating text; however, LLMs like GPT-3 and ChatGPT only implement the decoder module, simplifying their structure.
Large datasets consisting of billions of words are essential for pretraining LLMs effectively.
The general pretraining task for models similar to GPT is designed to predict the next word in a sentence. These LLMs exhibit emergent properties such as text classification, translation, or summarization capabilities beyond their initial training objective.
Once an LLM has been pretrained, it can be fine-tuned more efficiently for various downstream tasks using custom datasets.
Fine-tuning LLMs on specific datasets allows them to outperform general models in particular applications or domains.
